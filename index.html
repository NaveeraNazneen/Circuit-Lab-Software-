4Find the resistance of a galvanometer

Aim

To find the internal resistance (G) of a galvanometer by the half deflection method, and its figure of merit (k).

What you need

A galvanometer, a 2 V cell with very small internal resistance, a high resistance box R (0 to 50 000 Ω), a low resistance box S (0 to 300 Ω), two one-way keys K1 and K2, connecting wires and sandpaper.

Theory

In simple words

A galvanometer is a tiny current detector. When current flows, its needle swings. Inside is a coil of thin wire, and every wire pushes back a little against current. That push-back is called resistance. For the coil we call it G.

Imagine current as cars on a road. The galvanometer is a narrow gate. How narrow is the gate? Here is the trick:

  1. Let the cars through the gate. The needle swings, say 30 divisions.
  2. Build a second gate S right next to it. Cars can use either gate.
  3. Change S until the needle drops to exactly half, 15 divisions. Half the cars now use the new gate.
  4. Cars split into equal halves only when both gates are equally easy. So S is the same size as G.

Why only "almost"? The big resistor R works like a traffic controller. It keeps the total traffic nearly the same when we open the second gate. The bigger R is, the closer S gets to G. The exact formula is below.

Only K1 closed: Ig = E / (R + G) (deflection θ) K1 and K2 closed: Ig' = E·S / [R(G + S) + G·S] (deflection θ/2) Put Ig' = Ig / 2 → G = R·S / (R − S) If R is much bigger than S: G ≈ S Figure of merit: k = E / [(R + G)·θ] (current for 1 division) Biggest safe current: Ig(max) = n·k (n = divisions on each side)

Circuit diagram and simulator

Tap the keys in the circuit or use the buttons. The galvanometer in this simulator has a hidden resistance. Find it, then check yourself.

Procedure

In the real lab

  1. Draw the circuit and connect it. Make sure all plugs of the resistance boxes are tight.
  2. Take out a high resistance from R, close only K1 and adjust R until the deflection θ is large, an even number and inside the scale (say 30).
  3. Note R and θ.
  4. Close K2. Without changing R, adjust S until the deflection is exactly θ/2. Note S.
  5. Remove the K2 plug. Repeat steps 2 to 4 for five different values of R.
  6. Calculate G = RS/(R − S) for each set and take the mean.

In this simulator

  1. Close K1. Tap "Set R for 30 divisions".
  2. Read θ (the box "θ with K2 open").
  3. Close K2. Change S until the needle reads θ/2. Use the small ± buttons for fine tuning.
  4. Tap "Record reading".
  5. Change R a little (for example 24 000, 25 000, 26 000 Ω), open K2, and repeat. Take 5 readings.

Precautions

  • Plugs of the resistance boxes must be tight and clean.
  • The cell must have very small internal resistance (a fresh cell or a battery eliminator).
  • Always keep a high resistance in R before closing K1. A small R can burn the galvanometer.
  • Take the deflection as an even number so that half of it is easy to read.
  • Read the pointer straight on, to avoid parallax error.

Viva questions

Why do we use a very high resistance R?

It keeps the total current almost the same when S is connected, and it protects the galvanometer. It also makes G almost equal to S.

Can we use one third deflection instead of half?

Yes. Then G = 2RS/(R − 2S), which is about 2S when R is large.

Why must the cell have almost zero internal resistance?

The formula assumes the total resistance of the circuit is only R plus the galvanometer. A large internal resistance would change that and give an error in G.

What is the figure of merit?

It is the current needed to move the needle by one division. A smaller figure of merit means a more sensitive galvanometer.

5Verify Ohm's law

Aim

To verify Ohm's law (V = IR) by measuring the voltage, current and resistance in a circuit, using different resistors and different voltages.

What you need

A variable DC source (battery eliminator, 0 to 12 V), a few resistors, an ammeter, a voltmeter, a one-way key, connecting wires, sandpaper and graph paper.

Theory

In simple words

Think of a water pipe. Voltage (V) is the push of the water pump. Current (I) is how much water flows each second. Resistance (R) is how narrow or rough the pipe is.

  • Push twice as hard and twice as much water flows.
  • Use a narrower pipe and less water flows.

That is Ohm's law: V = I × R. If you draw a graph of V against I for a resistor you get a straight line starting at zero. The steepness (slope) of the line is the resistance.

Careful: a light bulb gets hot and its resistance changes, so its graph bends. Try the bulb in the simulator.

V = I × R R = V / I Graph of V (up) against I (across): straight line through zero Slope of the line = R Ammeter goes in SERIES. Voltmeter goes in PARALLEL.

Circuit diagram and simulator

Procedure

In the real lab

  1. Check the zero error of the ammeter and voltmeter.
  2. Connect the source, key, ammeter and resistor in series. Connect the voltmeter across the resistor.
  3. Close the key. Set the voltage to a small value. Note V and I.
  4. Increase the voltage step by step and note 6 readings.
  5. Plot a graph of V against I and find the slope.
  6. Repeat for other resistors. Try a bulb as well.

In this simulator

  1. Close the key. Pick a resistor.
  2. Move the voltage slider and tap "Add reading". Do this 6 times.
  3. Look at the graph and the V/I column. Is V/I constant?
  4. Try the mystery resistor: use the slope to find its value, then reveal it.
  5. Switch to the bulb and see what changes.

Precautions

  • Connect the ammeter in series and the voltmeter in parallel. Connect + to + and − to −.
  • Switch off the key between readings. A hot resistor changes its resistance.
  • Do not exceed the current rating of the resistor.
  • Use ammeter and voltmeter ranges that give a deflection of more than half the scale.

Viva questions

Why is the V–I graph a straight line?

Because V/I stays constant for an ohmic resistor, so V is directly proportional to I.

Why should an ammeter have very low resistance and a voltmeter very high resistance?

The ammeter must not reduce the current it measures, and the voltmeter must not take current away from the part of the circuit it is measuring.

Name something that does not obey Ohm's law.

A bulb filament, a diode or a thermistor. Their resistance changes with temperature or voltage.

What happens to a metal wire's resistance when it heats up?

It increases, because the atoms vibrate more and block the moving electrons more.

6Turn a galvanometer into a voltmeter

Aim

To convert a galvanometer (of known resistance G and figure of merit k) into a voltmeter of a given range, for example 0 to 3 V, and to verify it.

What you need

A galvanometer, a high resistance box, a battery, a rheostat (used as a potential divider), a standard voltmeter of the same range, a one-way key, wires and sandpaper.

Theory

In simple words

A galvanometer is like a very delicate weighing scale that can only weigh a feather: it handles only a tiny current. A big voltage would push far too much current through it.

So we add a long bumpy road in front of it: a large resistor R in series. Now even a big voltage pushes only a tiny, safe current through the galvanometer. We then relabel the scale in volts.

A voltmeter is connected across the thing you measure, so it must not steal current. That is why its total resistance must be very high.

Full-scale current: Ig = n × k For range V: V = Ig × (G + R) Series resistance: R = V / Ig − G Reading in volts = θ × (V / n) (one division = V / n volts)

Circuit diagram and simulator

Start by tapping "Use calculated R". Then slide the voltage and compare the converted reading with the standard voltmeter. Try a wrong R to see the error.

Procedure

In the real lab

  1. Find G and k of the galvanometer (Experiment 4). Calculate Ig = n·k.
  2. Calculate R = V/Ig − G for the range you want.
  3. Connect the resistance box with this R in series with the galvanometer.
  4. Connect this combination in parallel with a standard voltmeter, across the potential divider.
  5. Slide the rheostat contact and take 6 readings covering the whole range.
  6. Convert each deflection into volts and compare with the standard voltmeter.

In this simulator

  1. Enter G, k and the range you want.
  2. Read the calculated R. Tap "Use calculated R".
  3. Close K1. Move the voltage slider.
  4. Tap "Record reading" at 6 different voltages.
  5. Check that the error stays very small.

Precautions

  • Use a high resistance box. Keep the plugs tight.
  • Use the rheostat as a potential divider and start from zero voltage.
  • Connect the voltmeter in parallel. Check the polarity.
  • For verification use a standard voltmeter with the same range.

Viva questions

Why is the resistance connected in series?

A series resistance takes most of the voltage, so only a small safe current flows through the galvanometer, and the total resistance becomes very high.

What is the resistance of an ideal voltmeter?

Infinite. A real one is just very high.

If we increase R, what happens to the range?

The range increases (it can measure bigger voltages), but each division stands for more volts, so it is less sensitive.

Why must a voltmeter be connected in parallel?

Voltage is measured between two points, so the meter must connect to both points without breaking the circuit.

7Turn a galvanometer into an ammeter

Aim

To convert a galvanometer (of known resistance G and figure of merit k) into an ammeter of a given range, for example 0 to 30 mA, and to verify it.

What you need

A galvanometer, a shunt (a resistance box or a piece of wire of the right length), a battery, a rheostat, a standard ammeter of the same range, a one-way key, wires and sandpaper.

Theory

In simple words

The galvanometer is a narrow village lane. Only a few cars fit. To measure heavy traffic, build a wide express highway next to it. This highway is the shunt S, a small resistance connected in parallel.

Most cars take the highway and only a safe few use the lane. The needle only sees the few cars in the lane, but because we know the split, we can relabel the scale for the full traffic.

An ammeter goes in the middle of the road (in series) and counts every car, so it must be very easy to pass through. That is why an ammeter has a very small resistance.

Current in galvanometer: Ig = n × k Current in shunt: I − Ig Same voltage on both: Ig × G = (I − Ig) × S Shunt resistance: S = Ig × G / (I − Ig) Reading in amperes = θ × (I / n) (one division = I / n)

Circuit diagram and simulator

Tap "Use calculated S". Slide the current and compare the converted ammeter with the standard ammeter.

Procedure

In the real lab

  1. Find G and k (Experiment 4). Calculate Ig = n·k.
  2. Calculate S = Ig·G/(I − Ig) for the range I you want.
  3. Connect S in parallel with the galvanometer.
  4. Connect this converted ammeter in series with a standard ammeter, a rheostat, a key and a cell.
  5. Change the current with the rheostat. Take 6 readings.
  6. Convert deflections into amperes and compare.

In this simulator

  1. Enter G, k and the range you want.
  2. Tap "Use calculated S".
  3. Close K1 and slide the current.
  4. Tap "Record reading" 6 times.
  5. See how the current splits between G and S.

Precautions

  • The ammeter must be in series. Never connect it directly across a battery.
  • The shunt must have the correct value. If you use wire, its length must be exact.
  • Start with the rheostat at maximum resistance so the current is small.
  • Use a standard ammeter of the same range for verification.

Viva questions

Why is the shunt connected in parallel?

So that most of the current bypasses the galvanometer and only a safe amount passes through it.

What is the resistance of an ideal ammeter?

Zero. The combined resistance of G and S is G·S/(G + S), which is smaller than S.

Why does a milliammeter have more resistance than an ammeter made from the same galvanometer?

A smaller range needs a larger shunt, and a larger shunt gives a larger combined resistance.

What happens if the ammeter is connected in parallel?

Its very small resistance makes a near short circuit. A huge current flows and the meter or the source can be damaged.

8Series, parallel and logic gates

Aim

(a) To study series and parallel combinations of resistors and capacitors and verify the formulas.

(b) To verify the truth tables of logic gates.

What you need

Resistors, capacitors, a 6 V DC source, a multimeter and an LCR meter (or capacitance meter), a breadboard and wires.

Theory

In simple words

Resistors in series are like one road with many speed bumps. Every bump slows you down, so the bumps add up: R = R1 + R2 + ...

Resistors in parallel are like several roads open at once. More roads make travel easier, so the total is smaller than the smallest road.

Capacitors are like water tanks that store charge. They behave the opposite way. Tanks side by side (parallel) give one bigger tank, so they add. Tanks in series are harder to fill and the total is smaller than the smallest tank.

Resistors in series: R = R1 + R2 + R3 Resistors in parallel: 1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3 Capacitors in parallel: C = C1 + C2 + C3 Capacitors in series: 1/C = 1/C1 + 1/C2 + 1/C3 Capacitor charge: Q = C × V (series: same Q, parallel: same V)

Circuit diagram and simulator

Procedure

In the real lab

  1. Measure each resistor (or capacitor) alone with the meter.
  2. Connect them in series. Measure the total.
  3. Connect them in parallel. Measure the total.
  4. Calculate the total with the formula and compare with the meter.

In this simulator

  1. Pick resistors or capacitors, series or parallel, and how many.
  2. Type your own values.
  3. Work out the total on paper, enter it in "Check my answer".
  4. Tap "Record reading" to fill the table.

What you need

A digital trainer kit (or a breadboard with a 5 V supply), the ICs 7408 (AND), 7432 (OR), 7404 (NOT), 7400 (NAND), 7402 (NOR) and 7486 (XOR), two switches, an LED with a 330 Ω resistor and connecting wires.

Theory

In simple words

Computers only understand two things: 1 (switch ON, about 5 V) and 0 (switch OFF, about 0 V). A logic gate is a small decision maker. It looks at its input switches and decides whether the output light should be ON or OFF.

  • AND: the light is ON only if switch A and switch B are ON. Like two keys needed to open a safe.
  • OR: the light is ON if A or B (or both) is ON. Like two doors into the same room.
  • NOT: the opposite. Input 1 gives 0. Input 0 gives 1.
  • NAND: NOT of AND. It is ON for everything except both switches ON.
  • NOR: NOT of OR. It is ON only when both switches are OFF.
  • XOR: ON when the two inputs are different.
  • XNOR: ON when the two inputs are the same.

A truth table lists every possible input and the output you should get.

Circuit diagram and simulator

Procedure

In the real lab

  1. Place the IC on the trainer or breadboard.
  2. Connect +5 V to pin 14 and ground to pin 7.
  3. Connect the inputs to the two switches and the output to the LED (with the 330 Ω resistor).
  4. Try all four input combinations and note whether the LED is ON (1) or OFF (0).
  5. Compare with the truth table. Repeat for each gate.

In this simulator

  1. Choose a gate.
  2. Tap the input boxes A and B to set 0 or 1.
  3. Visit all rows. The table ticks each row you have tested.
  4. Try "Mystery gate": test it and guess which gate it is.

Precautions

  • Never connect the IC power the wrong way round (pin 14 is +5 V, pin 7 is ground).
  • Always use a resistor in series with an LED.
  • Do not leave inputs floating. Connect them to 0 V or 5 V.
  • For capacitors, discharge them before measuring. Electrolytic capacitors have a + and − side.

Viva questions

Why is the parallel resistance smaller than the smallest resistor?

Each extra path gives the current one more way to flow, so the overall resistance falls.

Why are the capacitor formulas opposite to the resistor formulas?

Capacitors in parallel increase the plate area, so the capacitance adds. In series the plate gap effectively grows, so the total capacitance drops.

Which gates are called universal gates?

NAND and NOR. Any other gate can be built using only one of them.

What voltage means logic 1 in a TTL IC?

About 5 V (anything above roughly 2 V counts as high). Logic 0 is about 0 V (below roughly 0.8 V).
